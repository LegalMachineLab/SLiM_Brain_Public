# Ordinary meaning interpretation

_Status: emergent; family: legal_task._

## Definition

Determining the ordinary or communicative meaning of legal text (statutes, contracts, insurance policies) as an interpretive task, and the evidence — intuition, dictionaries, corpora, language models — offered for it (motivating claims: CLM-0021-001, CLM-0021-004, CLM-0021-015).

Conceptual claims on this concept, each with its source:
- Lee and Egbert (2025): LLM chatbot responses to ordinary-meaning questions are neither a corpus linguistic analysis of natural language use nor a survey of human intuitions about ordinary meaning; at most they present the intuitions of a single artificial entity, functioning as an artificial expert witness that simulates one well-read human, and those artificial intuitions are no more transparent, replicable, or generalizable than the intuitions of an ordinary person or a judge. [CLM-0021-002]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): The core case for modern textualism, that a careful judicial investigation into ordinary communicative content constrains judicial discretion, rests on three pillars, transparency, replicability, and generalizability, and the viability of any tool offering evidence of empirical linguistic fact should be measured by the degree to which it lives up to those three standards. [CLM-0021-003]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): Ordinary-meaning questions of the kind presented in Snell and DeLeon are empirical questions of linguistic fact, since ordinary meaning is a distributional fact about which usage predominates, and empirical questions call for replicable evidence produced by transparent methods rather than bare human intuition or an arbitrary preference for one dictionary definition over another. [CLM-0021-004]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): The word 'ordinary' in the ordinary-meaning rule carries two distinct dimensions, a speech-community dimension (the vernacular of the general public rather than the jargon of a technical field) and an empirical dimension (how a term is typically or normally used within that community); the breadth of LLM training data speaks at most to the first, and tells nothing about the ability of LLMs to produce evidence on the second, which is the question actually in dispute in Snell and DeLeon. [CLM-0021-015]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): Serial prompts to one chatbot do not amount to a survey of separate speakers: any given AI rests on a single LLM, one 'brain', so variation across its responses reflects the variability of a single person repeatedly asked the same question, not the variability between individuals in a population; proponents must decide whether a single response is the (black-box) empirical result or whether multiple responses form a sample, since it cannot work both ways and both approaches present serious problems. [CLM-0021-019]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Direct querying of LLMs creates a risk of 'prompt shopping' (or 'prejudiced prompting') parallel to dictionary shopping: by merely adjusting how queries are framed, an interpreter can, consciously or unwittingly, elicit substantially different interpretations of the same legal text, and the current language-modelling paradigm offers no inherent mechanism to mitigate this. There is therefore no compelling reason to believe that LLMs, as employed through the direct-query method, offer any meaningful advantage over traditional interpretive tools in managing bias; they may simply add a new vector for selective interpretation, undermining the promised objectivity, consistency, predictability and transparency. [CLM-0022-009]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Expressions of the form 'Adj-ly restrained' are systematically indeterminate between an Adverb-as-Means reading (the entity is restrained by an external agent through Adj means; 'restrained' as a passive participle verb) and an Adverb-as-Domain reading (the entity exhibits self-imposed restraint in the Adj domain of behaviour; 'restrained' as a gradable adjective), owing to a lexical ambiguity of 'restrained'. 'Physically restrained' exhibits this ambiguity, so as a matter of ordinary meaning without further context it is linguistically indeterminate, and corporeality is a necessary implication of only one of its readings. [CLM-0022-014]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Judge Newsom's 'standard approach' to ordinary meaning, which combines 'constituent part splitting' (interpreting each term of a legal text in isolation) with 'abstract metalinguistic querying' (identifying a term's in-context meaning with what speakers would call its ordinary meaning in the abstract), gets the right answer only where a term has a generally stable, context-invariant meaning and fails in many other cases, such as 'vehicle' in differently worded park rules. This exposes a tension between textualism's commitment to context-sensitive interpretation and approaches that posit a free-floating 'ordinary meaning' of terms. [CLM-0022-016]
- Miller (2025): Generative AI's utility exceeds that of dictionaries in its ability to address basic textual nuance, such as idioms, metaphors, and composite phrases like "physically restrained", and to grapple with ambiguity through inter-word relationships, whereas dictionaries may only be equipped to shed light on the isolated meaning of individual words. [CLM-0039-020]

Aliases: textualism, empirical inquiry into ordinary meaning.

## Claims about the concept

### Descriptive

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) state that Textualist case law offers no single orthodox answer to how common a usage must be to count as 'ordinary': courts sometimes treat a meaning as ordinary if it is merely attested or permissible and elsewhere limit 'ordinary' to a relatively common, most common, or prototypical meaning; nor is there a single answer on whether ordinary meaning refers to a term's conceptual reach or to how the term is ordinarily applied. [CLM-0021-005]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: case_law (unclear); positive form: split
- Lee and Egbert (2025) state that Since legal scholars began exploring corpus linguistic tools for interpreting the language of law about fifteen years before the time of writing, those tools have been featured in dozens of scholarly articles, numerous judicial opinions, and extensive amicus briefs, and courts have relied on corpus data far and wide. [CLM-0021-007]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: case_law (unclear); positive form: frequency
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Textualism's methodological toolkit is shifting: the newest textualist Justices are growing more sceptical of traditional tools (dictionaries, linguistic canons, substantive canons) and turning to new tools (corpus linguistics and surveys), and the vast majority of textualist scholars and judges treat the study of ordinary meaning as a partly empirical question, which makes it sensible to ask whether LLM-based chatbots might also feature in future textualism. [CLM-0022-020]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: case_law (unclear); positive form: trend
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that This is the first article to assess the emerging judicial practice of directly querying LLM chatbots for ordinary meaning in equal measure from computer science, linguistics and law; the only other sustained scholarly analysis of the Snell and Deleon opinions (Lee and Egbert) proceeds from corpus linguistics and reaches a diverging evaluation. [CLM-0022-021]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature

**US, CO, IN, PK, AR, PE, MX, NL, GB**

- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Judges have begun using LLM chatbots in legal interpretation and other judicial tasks: a U.S. circuit judge (Judge Newsom of the Eleventh Circuit) has twice used chatbots in concurring opinions to aid textualist interpretation of the ordinary meaning of an insurance contract (Snell) and of the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines (Deleon), and judges have used chatbots to interpret law in Colombia, India and Pakistan and for other judicial purposes in Argentina, Peru, Mexico, the Netherlands and the U.K. [CLM-0022-018]. — jurisdiction: US, CO, IN, PK, AR, PE, MX, NL, GB (cumulative); basis: case_law (dictum); positive form: existence

**general**

- Waldon et al. (2025) state that The LLMs that power chatbots are, by design, trained on specialised, non-naturalistic data that cannot plausibly be taken to reflect everyday linguistic usage: synthetic instruction data, preference data recording human-computer or computer-computer exchanges, and machine-generated text that spills into pre-training corpora. Because instruction tuning and preference alignment follow pre-training, LLMs are optimised to predict this artificial data even if they were initially pre-trained on naturalistic text. [CLM-0022-003]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Instruction-tuning data and preference data often encode judgments about language itself, so a chatbot queried for judgments about ordinary language reflects the linguistic judgments of the researchers and annotators who created that data, and those judgments are sometimes substandard. This problematises the assumption that fine-tuning steps help LLMs produce correct statements about ordinary language, and claims about LLMs' linguistic capabilities should be approached with heightened scepticism. [CLM-0022-004]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Even with perfectly curated fine-tuning data, the direct-query method is flawed because it mistakenly assumes that LLMs can faithfully articulate plain-language characterisations of their underlying linguistic generalisations. LLM chatbots answering metalinguistic questions are pseudolinguists rather than artificial linguists: their stated judgments about language are poorly calibrated to what the models have actually learned about the statistics of language, so direct querying is a coarse and unreliable means of probing the linguistic knowledge LLMs encode. [CLM-0022-005]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: literature
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Direct chatbot queries are not reproducible: chatbot responses depend on hidden system prompts that developers may change unobserved, on prior exchanges and cross-session chat history, and on sampling randomness governed by seed and temperature settings that may be inaccessible to users. Consequently, even the disclosures Judge Newsom made in Snell and Deleon (exact prompts, models, number of queries) are not enough to fully reproduce his procedures. [CLM-0022-007]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that With respect to conscious or unconscious human bias, LLMs may pose greater risks than traditional interpretive tools: automation bias and users' tendency to anthropomorphise LLM platforms encourage over-trust in outputs, judges are less equipped to recognise manipulation of LLM responses than selective dictionary use, and automated prompt-optimisation tools could let judges, lawyers or scholars generate neutral-sounding prompts designed to elicit particular interpretive outcomes. [CLM-0022-010]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature
- Waldon et al. (2025) state that Closed-source chatbot platforms rely on inaccessible training data, non-transparent models and hidden system prompts and change constantly under opaque circumstances, so it is not possible to assess the underlying LLM's ability to reflect ordinary linguistic meaning; a closed model cannot be 'called to the stand' for re-questioning by dissenting views or on appeal, making decisions based on its outputs inscrutable. Fully open-source solutions are the only ones that enable fully transparent reporting of LLM interactions. [CLM-0022-012]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Interpretive

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) read United States v. DeLeon, 116 F.4th 1260 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: Judge Newsom's identification in DeLeon of a 'common core' across the varying responses to his serial AI queries is not the result of a transparent, replicable experiment about ordinary meaning but the product of a subjective determination by an individual judge of what should count as the core ordinary meaning, because 'common core' is not operationalized in a transparent, replicable way and the conclusion glosses over the quantitative questions embedded in it. [CLM-0021-020]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) read U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Manual § 2B3.1(b)(4)(B) as follows: The context of U.S. Sentencing Guidelines §2B3.1(b)(4) resolves the indeterminacy of 'physically restrained' in favour of the Adverb-as-Means reading: the parallel with 'abducted' in subsection (A), the Guidelines' own definition of the phrase as forcible restraint, and the implausible breadth of the Adverb-as-Domain reading all point that way. Hence §2B3.1(b)(4)(B) requires 'corporeality' not because that notion is inherent in the ordinary meaning of the phrase but because it is a necessary implication of the phrase in context, and the statutory definition is not in tension with ordinary meaning but additional contextual evidence of the intended resolution. [CLM-0022-015]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: legislation
- Miller (2025) read Snell v. United Specialty Ins. Co., 102 F.4th 1208 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: Judge Newsom's reasoning that generative AI's predictive responses, patterned on extensive and varied use of language, could inform ordinary meaning makes intuitive sense and matches the value that any contemporary dictionary could provide. [CLM-0039-002]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) read Snell v. United Specialty Ins. Co., 102 F.4th 1208 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: In Snell v. United Specialty Insurance Co., Judge Newsom's two prompts were likely entered in the same chat, so that the model's conversational memory caused the second response to be heavily influenced by the first; the ordering of the prompts likely injected significant bias into the results by tacitly accepting the model's initial assertion. [CLM-0039-006]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: case_law (dictum)
- Miller (2025) read Snell v. United Specialty Ins. Co., 102 F.4th 1208 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: The primary issue with Judge Newsom's prompting method in Snell was the small number of responses; because generative AI responses vary, using a few responses as an alternative definition for comparison against other sources is significantly devalued. [CLM-0039-008]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) read United States v. Deleon, 116 F.4th 1260 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: Ancillary patterns that recur in large-batch responses without being ubiquitous, such as "blocking one's path" as a form of physical restraint, which appeared verbatim in four of the Claude responses in United States v. Deleon, may have significant bearing on ordinary meaning and should not be cleaved away as creative fuzz distracting from the common core; a judge may prudently treat such results as requiring further, more specific evidence such as legislative history. [CLM-0039-011]. — jurisdiction: US; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) read Ordinary meaning interpretation as follows: Weighed against the general unfamiliarity of new methods and the subjectivity inherent in LLM construction, the public accessibility of generative AI and the ability to manage some subjectivity through standardized interpretive techniques and judicial communication give reason to think that consulting generative AI offers superior transparency benefits (predictability and fair notice) over dictionaries when appropriate, since dictionaries themselves leave something to be desired on transparency. [CLM-0039-019]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) read Snell v. United Specialty Ins. Co., 102 F.4th 1208 (11th Cir. 2024) as follows: Consulting multiple generative AI models of reasonable quality, as Judge Newsom did in Snell, is an important initial step in present circumstances because it accesses varied corpora and a more nuanced understanding of ordinary meaning, but the approach is almost certainly subject to diminishing returns, since engaging ever more diverse models risks using subpar software trained on smaller bodies of text. [CLM-0039-025]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

### Normative

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Corpus linguistic tools are, to date, the one main set of tools that can produce transparent, replicable, and generalizable empirical evidence relevant to ordinary-meaning questions like those in Snell and DeLeon, and judges should continue to rely on corpus data in place of LLM AI queries. [CLM-0021-006]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Accessibility, low cost, ease of use, and the growing adoption of LLM AIs in the legal profession are not sufficient grounds for adopting them as sources of evidence of ordinary meaning; because their responses are bare opinions rather than empirical facts, the 'good enough' snowball effect is troubling, as judges may be impressed by responses that confirm their own experience or be so drawn in by the seductive appeal of the AI's views as to give them controlling weight subconsciously. [CLM-0021-023]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Asking an AI to analyze the quantitative results of corpus coding and report whether the ordinary meaning of a term includes a given element would be a step too far, too close to robo-judging, because it would outsource the judicial decisions about what ordinary meaning is, how much evidence suffices, and how the data should be weighted; judges do not need to be told the ordinary meaning of a word or phrase by a human or a computer but need transparent empirical evidence of how words and phrases are commonly used. [CLM-0021-027]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Judges should not rely on direct queries to LLM chatbots (questions of the form 'what is the ordinary meaning of [term]?') as evidence of the ordinary meaning of legal texts; the direct-query method, as currently practised, is unsound. [CLM-0022-001]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Judges could use LLM chatbots as dialectical partners, prompting them to generate and critique competing arguments about meaning (brainstorming), and treating the outputs not as evidence of ordinary meaning but as potential considerations for the judge's own linguistic analysis. This retains judicial sovereignty over interpretive reasoning; used in this way, variability across LLM responses is an asset rather than a liability, and even closed-source platforms can constructively inform decision-making. [CLM-0022-002]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Textualists should give up the notion of domain-invariant 'ordinary meanings', favour interpretive tools that are sensitive to context (such as some linguistic canons like ejusdem generis), and be wary of tools premised on domain-invariant meanings: they should not directly query a chatbot about a term's ordinary meaning, should not rely heavily on a dictionary definition as establishing ordinary meaning across contexts, and the same scepticism extends to surveys of many people about a term's abstract ordinary meaning. [CLM-0022-017]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Although generative AI responses do seem informative of ordinary meaning to an extent similar to that described by Judge Newsom, the practical issues with generative AI (hallucinations, the temporal relevance of training corpora, and private control over the models), taken together, cannot currently be sufficiently mitigated to advise its regular, widespread adoption for textual interpretation. [CLM-0039-001]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Judges who use generative AI for interpretation should be required to preregister research plans specifying the models, versions, prompts, and any temperature or parameter alterations, and to give a supplemental rationale for any deviation; preregistration eliminates unexplained subjectivity in selecting between models and results, though it raises unresolved practicality concerns about who would police the plans and how contested deviations would be reviewed. [CLM-0039-003]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Generative AI interpretation should be standardized to a narrow range of high-quality models with an established track record, trained on data too expansive to easily alter (such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude), using the most current version freely available to the public, so that the public has access to the models used by the court; a judge's decision to go beyond that subset should be rationalized in the preregistered research plan. [CLM-0039-004]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Those using repetitive prompts for interpretive research should use distinct conversational strings and clear the model's memory between prompts to prevent new responses being contaminated by previous ones, except where the interpreter intends to apply context (a request for technical meaning, temporal or geographical limits, or the form of writing in which the term appears) to a definition the model has already supplied. [CLM-0039-007]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that The simple application of a single generated definition is insufficient to shed light on an interpretive question even under the best circumstances; generative AI should instead be used to generate large numbers of ordinary-meaning responses and to flag conceptual patterns between them, so that the variability of responses becomes an asset revealing facets of ordinary meaning that might escape the interpreter's intuitions, with the interpreter weighing the value of those patterns. [CLM-0039-009]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that A response generated with minimized temperature should not be used in isolation as evidence of ordinary meaning, since that again treats a changeable source as a dictionary; it may instead serve as a control condition against which large-batch prompting results are compared, but only when generated on the same model as those results, so researchers should provide a minimized-temperature response for each model they access. [CLM-0039-015]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Judges using generative AI for interpretation should be required to export the results of their investigation as a digital file providing a clear transcript of the research process, so that interested parties can compare the results against the preregistered research plan and detect deviation; this serves as an additional layer of public reassurance reinforcing the credibility of judicial results rather than ensuring their accuracy, and parties submitting their own generative AI results should likewise be required to export complete, consolidated accounts. [CLM-0039-017]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that The danger that generative AI companies will manipulate their models as litigants is overstated, since a judge fearing manipulation by a party can simply refrain from using the tool under that party's control; the true potential danger lies in backroom deals between generative AI firms and well-funded future litigants purchasing interpretive strength, so judges should look not just to the parties of the case but to its financial stakes and to the resources currently required to alter a given model. [CLM-0039-022]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that The concern that ordinary meanings derived from modern text have little bearing on decades-old documents may be less compelling given competing temporal issues with other interpretive tools, since dictionaries are updated too infrequently and corpus linguistics loses value as fewer texts fall within the temporal parameters; nevertheless the temporal dynamic should not be ignored, and judges using generative AI should be cautious where linguistic drift might exist. [CLM-0039-024]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**general**

- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Until AI interpretability research has progressed far enough to say what a given model has learned about English from its training data, there is no fully responsible way to use LLMs as proxies for ordinary meaning, because without knowing what LLMs learn one cannot measure whether and to what extent their linguistic competence diverges from ordinary competence. [CLM-0022-006]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Empirical

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that In a coded random sample of Corpus of Contemporary American English concordance lines, 'landscaping' is somewhat regularly used to encompass non-botanical improvements (68 of 347 determinate lines, 19.6%) and quite commonly used for improvements with a functional purpose (123 of 193 determinate lines, 63.7%); non-botanical improvements can therefore fall within 'landscaping', but the prototypical improvements are botanical. [CLM-0021-008]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that In the 14-billion-word iWeb corpus, of 8 category terms found describing work involving an in-ground trampoline, 7 were variations on 'landscaping' and 1 on 'construction', suggesting that landscaping is a common, if not the most common, term for such work; because the dataset is small and iWeb has not been fully evaluated for representativeness, the results are offered by way of example of how a corpus can address expected applications. [CLM-0021-009]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that In every codable concordance line for 'physically restrain' or 'physical restraint' in a random iWeb sample of 500 (81 determinate lines, 419 indeterminate), the phrase referred to a constraint imposed through bodily contact; the broad, non-bodily-contact notion of physically restrained was unattested. [CLM-0021-010]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that In the Corpus of Contemporary American English, references to using a gun to control another person are numerous (190) and none uses any form of 'restrain' (all use 'threaten'), whereas 'restrain' is overwhelmingly the verb used with objects that involve bodily contact such as rope, chain and handcuffs (55 of 65 references, 85%); this meaning-to-word evidence reinforces a narrow, bodily-contact notion of physically restrained, so the corpus study points unequivocally in favor of the narrow reading. [CLM-0021-011]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment

**geographical_proxy:US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that Queried 100 times with the same prompt used in the Snell concurrence, 'Is installing an in-ground trampoline landscaping?', ChatGPT gave three materially different answer types (an unqualified yes 68 times, 'could be, but not in the traditional sense' 18 times, and 'could be, if conditions are met' 14 times), which would lead users to different conclusions depending on how ordinary meaning is operationalized and whose lack of internal consistency raises serious questions about replicability. [CLM-0021-017]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment; temporal reference: June 2024
- Lee and Egbert (2025) report that ChatGPT's opinions on ordinary meaning are fragile and manipulable: when told that its confident answer that installing an in-ground trampoline can be landscaping was incorrect, it modified its answer, and when the original question was posed again it confidently stated the opposite with more concrete supporting evidence; a response based on empirical evidence could not and would not change merely because someone disagreed, since subjective opinions are subject to change and empirical facts are not. [CLM-0021-018]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment; temporal reference: June 2024
- Waldon et al. (2025) report that In a reproducible red-teaming exercise (open instruction-tuned Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410, temperature 0.1, fixed random seed, released code), Judge Newsom's original prompts about 'landscaping' and 'physically restrained' yielded completions largely consistent with the chatbot responses he reported, but subtle, seemingly neutral modifications of those prompts elicited the opposite metalinguistic judgments: that installing an in-ground trampoline is not typically 'landscaping', and that 'physically restrained' has a sense not requiring physical force. [CLM-0022-008]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Waldon et al. (2025) report that In a dialectical prompting exercise on the phrase disputed in Deleon, Ministral produced a fairly cohesive argument for a corporeality requirement of 'physically restrained' (with sophisticated pragmatic observations but unsupported usage generalisations and a hallucinated Merriam-Webster definition) and, in a fresh session, a counter-argument with an alternative analysis not requiring tangible force. The exercise led to the discovery of an ambiguity between a 'means' and a 'domain' interpretation that expert linguistic analysis can then develop. [CLM-0022-013]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Miller (2025) report that When the three-step large-batch prompting process was applied to the facts of Snell, ChatGPT failed to follow instructions: it omitted an analysis for one of the twenty definitions (Definition 16) under Step 3, and in an alternate run it consistently summarized rather than quoted its own definitions, applying its summary instead of the original definition for two of them. [CLM-0039-013]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment

[CLM-0022-014]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that Generative AI's utility exceeds that of dictionaries in its ability to address basic textual nuance, such as idioms, metaphors, and composite phrases like "physically restrained", and to grapple with ambiguity through inter-word relationships, whereas dictionaries may only be equipped to shed light on the isolated meaning of individual words. [CLM-0039-020]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

### Methodological

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Existing LLM-based AI tools are not up to the task of the empirical inquiry into ordinary meaning that modern textualism calls for: their outputs are a form of artificial intuition rather than empirical analysis, and they are in no position to produce reliable datapoints on questions such as whether installing an in-ground trampoline is 'landscaping' or whether holding a victim at gunpoint is 'physically restraining' him. [CLM-0021-001]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Corpus data provide the fine-grained detail, how common a given sense of a term is and how often the term is applied to a given referent, that lets a court articulate an operative standard of ordinary meaning and transparently apply it; AI chatbot responses instead give bottom-line conclusions hedged with words like 'can', 'generally' and 'typically' that skate over the embedded questions of the ordinary-meaning standard, delegate the selection of the standard to the AI, and so are not 'datapoints' to be considered alongside other evidence. [CLM-0021-012]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that A three-step large-batch prompting process, in which the model is asked in a single prompt for twenty ordinary meanings of the term at three sentences each, then asked which of those definitions would include a brief and neutrally phrased set of simple facts and why, and finally asked whether each answer would change if the term appeared in a stated form of writing such as an insurance policy or criminal statute, can be employed quickly by anyone with access to free versions of high-quality models, is fairly standardizable across cases, and relates favorably to transparency, utility, and practicality concerns. [CLM-0039-010]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that The large-batch prompting process, in isolation, is insufficient for determining interpretive questions: it does not consider the phrase's use within a sentence or list, does not directly address when a technical rather than ordinary meaning is called for, ignores external evidence such as legislative history or custom, leaves a small measure of discretion in framing the simple facts, and depends on models that may fail to follow directions. [CLM-0039-012]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Miller (2025) argue that The proportion of generated definitions under which a model includes a given fact pattern (for example, 13 of 20 definitions of "landscaping" including a below-ground trampoline) should not be taken as strong evidence for inclusion, because prompting the model the same way a second time would likely yield different analytical conclusions; the method relies on generative AI's capacity to detect patterns, not to perform thoughtful analysis, though specific and common patterns may be stronger evidence than general and infrequent ones. [CLM-0039-014]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**general**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that There is no basis for concluding that the LLMs behind widely used chatbots are representative of a speech community of ordinary language users: OpenAI's disclosures about training data say nothing about the target language domain, corpus design, or sampling decisions, and although the training sets are enormous, size alone cannot compensate for poor corpus design because representativeness is more strongly influenced by the quality of the sample than by its size. [CLM-0021-013]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Reinforcement learning from human feedback gives a further reason to doubt the representativeness of LLM outputs even if the training data were representative: a minuscule rater pool, reportedly numbering in the dozens and in one sample only 17% from the United States, adjusts responses in ways whose interaction with the training data the black-box nature of the model makes impossible to know, and rater inputs may have an outsized influence on responses to ordinary-meaning queries. [CLM-0021-014]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that The 'transparency' claimed for LLM AIs is only openness about the model selected, the prompt, and the temperature setting, or transparency relative to a dictionary-centric current practice, not the transparency of method and evidentiary basis that renders an inquiry replicable; because LLM AIs rest on black-box algorithms whose operations cannot be understood, evaluated, or modified, they fall short of the transparency and replicability an empirical linguistic tool requires. [CLM-0021-016]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Engel and McAdams have not established the accuracy of GPT responses as evidence of ordinary meaning: their 'belief prompt' plus Likert query was settled on by trial and error to align with Tobia's 'vehicles in the park' survey, it correlated highly only for objects that survey participants considered likely vehicles and less well for marginal ones, and because the prompts were developed and evaluated on the same survey data there is no way to know whether the same prompt-design principles would be appropriate for any other ordinary-meaning question. [CLM-0021-021]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that The proponents' responses to the hallucination problem fall short: the analogy to cross-checking Westlaw against Lexis is inapt because LLM AIs do not generate cross-verifiable answers to empirical questions with finite answers and may answer the same query differently at different times, and the comparison to hallucinating lawyers is what-about-ism, since a problem with one source of evidence is no reason to overlook it in another and lawyers are required to cite chapter and verse so that judges can check them. [CLM-0021-022]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that LLM AIs introduce significantly more discretion than best-practice corpus analysis, not less: in corpus linguistics the discretionary decisions (corpus selection, operationalization of the construct, searches evaluable for precision and recall, coding frameworks evaluated for inter-rater reliability, statistical analysis) are informed by linguistic theory and can be transparently reported and replicated, whereas chatbot users can make none of these decisions, cannot know how the chatbot operationalizes constructs or evaluate it for accuracy, and depend on prompt engineering that is highly discretionary and highly influential. [CLM-0021-024]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that The contention that corpus linguistics is inattentive to context, comparing only brief snippets of text rather than whole documents, is incorrect: corpus methods are as attentive to context as the researcher deems appropriate, since a search yields concordance lines whose context window can be expanded up to the entire source document with the window size and analytic standard transparently reported, and they are far more context-sensitive than dictionaries. [CLM-0021-025]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that LLM technology could be leveraged within corpus linguistic analysis without sacrificing transparency, replicability, and generalizability: corpus tools could adopt a conversational chatbot interface that asks clarifying questions, reports the exact operational definitions and methods used, and saves search settings so that the same search on the same corpus reproduces the same results; and an AI could be trained to apply a human-developed coding framework to concordance lines, with its accuracy evaluated against human coders and the approach abandoned if satisfactory accuracy is never reached. [CLM-0021-026]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Anyone using LLMs to aid legal interpretation should follow five best practices that encourage (though do not guarantee) robustness and reproducibility: (i) use open models and interfaces; (ii) document interaction settings, including model designation, version date, the complete interaction protocol, temperature, maximum output length, prior context and API details; (iii) use multiple models and prompts; (iv) independently verify specific claims in LLM output, because LLMs hallucinate; and (v) recruit domain experts in linguistics and AI. [CLM-0022-011]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

## Disagreements

- Waldon et al. (2025) holds: In a reproducible red-teaming exercise (open instruction-tuned Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410, temperature 0.1, fixed random seed, released code), Judge Newsom's original prompts about 'landscaping' and 'physically restrained' yielded completions largely consistent with the chatbot responses he reported, but subtle, seemingly neutral modifications of those prompts elicited the opposite metalinguistic judgments: that installing an in-ground trampoline is not typically 'landscaping', and that 'physically restrained' has a sense not requiring physical force. [CLM-0022-008]; Lee and Egbert (2025) holds: Queried 100 times with the same prompt used in the Snell concurrence, 'Is installing an in-ground trampoline landscaping?', ChatGPT gave three materially different answer types (an unqualified yes 68 times, 'could be, but not in the traditional sense' 18 times, and 'could be, if conditions are met' 14 times), which would lead users to different conclusions depending on how ordinary meaning is operationalized and whose lack of internal consistency raises serious questions about replicability. [CLM-0021-017]; note: Waldon et al. discuss Lee and Egbert's 100-query study by name and argue that its risks 'are mitigated by setting random seeds when sampling', so that the demonstration is not a compelling challenge to careful direct querying.
- Waldon et al. (2025) holds: In a reproducible red-teaming exercise (open instruction-tuned Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410, temperature 0.1, fixed random seed, released code), Judge Newsom's original prompts about 'landscaping' and 'physically restrained' yielded completions largely consistent with the chatbot responses he reported, but subtle, seemingly neutral modifications of those prompts elicited the opposite metalinguistic judgments: that installing an in-ground trampoline is not typically 'landscaping', and that 'physically restrained' has a sense not requiring physical force.
