# Rule of law and legitimacy

_Status: anchor; family: normative_concern._

## Definition

Legitimacy, authority, and rule-of-law implications of AI in legal interpretation and adjudication.

Conceptual claims on this concept, each with its source:
- Lee and Egbert (2025): The core case for modern textualism, that a careful judicial investigation into ordinary communicative content constrains judicial discretion, rests on three pillars, transparency, replicability, and generalizability, and the viability of any tool offering evidence of empirical linguistic fact should be measured by the degree to which it lives up to those three standards. [CLM-0021-003]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Direct querying of LLMs creates a risk of 'prompt shopping' (or 'prejudiced prompting') parallel to dictionary shopping: by merely adjusting how queries are framed, an interpreter can, consciously or unwittingly, elicit substantially different interpretations of the same legal text, and the current language-modelling paradigm offers no inherent mechanism to mitigate this. There is therefore no compelling reason to believe that LLMs, as employed through the direct-query method, offer any meaningful advantage over traditional interpretive tools in managing bias; they may simply add a new vector for selective interpretation, undermining the promised objectivity, consistency, predictability and transparency. [CLM-0022-009]
- Horner et al. (2025): Every formal encoding of a legal provision is an interpretation, and a true gold standard would have to correspond to the authentic interpretation, which only the judiciary can provide and only for provisions actually disputed in court; therefore in some jurisdictions a true gold standard for legal formalization cannot exist, and any gold standard further depends on the coders' understanding of legal intent, context and encoding style. [CLM-0032-012]
- Gridin (2026): The same deterministic architecture that makes AI the most powerful tool for legal transparency is, without procedural constraints, equally capable of becoming an instrument of systematic surveillance and arbitrary control; whether a system functions as a transparency mechanism or a surveillance apparatus is determined not by the technology but by three design choices - a Digital Warrant governing biometric access, an Algorithmic Due Process escalation matrix, and distributed multi-signature governance - so the choice is between constitutional philosophies of governance, not between technologies. [CLM-0049-043]

### Normative

**GB**

- Briggs of Westbourne (2026) argue that Procedural rules and regulation to accommodate AI in civil justice ought to be developed in advance, by defining the objectives and principles behind the rules prospectively and in a transparent environment open to public scrutiny, rather than by responding piecemeal to technological developments as they occur, as happened with digitisation. This does not require having all the answers upfront. [CLM-0047-001]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Briggs of Westbourne (2026) argue that Whether AI can or should replace human lawyers and judges in the resolution of some or all types of civil litigation, and what precisely the courts should use AI for, is a democratic question: it is not appropriate for lawyers and judges to decide, but is a matter for the people, and for taxpayers in particular, who must decide what they want as the arbiter of their disputes and what they are prepared to pay for the human elements in the process. [CLM-0047-012]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Briggs of Westbourne (2026) argue that There will need to be complete transparency about how far AI is assisting, or in certain areas replacing, the work of human judges, so that what actually happens behind the scenes does not get out of step with public expectations. [CLM-0047-016]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Briggs of Westbourne (2026) argue that Before it is too late, it is necessary to identify what contribution the human elements of the civil justice process make to the rule of law, which human qualities will never be supplanted by robots, and what red lines are needed to preserve that human contribution from erosion by AI; judges should not stay silent on this even though whether they are replaced is a democratic question. [CLM-0047-017]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**US**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Accessibility, low cost, ease of use, and the growing adoption of LLM AIs in the legal profession are not sufficient grounds for adopting them as sources of evidence of ordinary meaning; because their responses are bare opinions rather than empirical facts, the 'good enough' snowball effect is troubling, as judges may be impressed by responses that confirm their own experience or be so drawn in by the seductive appeal of the AI's views as to give them controlling weight subconsciously. [CLM-0021-023]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Asking an AI to analyze the quantitative results of corpus coding and report whether the ordinary meaning of a term includes a given element would be a step too far, too close to robo-judging, because it would outsource the judicial decisions about what ordinary meaning is, how much evidence suffices, and how the data should be weighted; judges do not need to be told the ordinary meaning of a word or phrase by a human or a computer but need transparent empirical evidence of how words and phrases are commonly used. [CLM-0021-027]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**general**

- T.Y.S.S. et al. (2024) argue that Because legal systems are human-centric and human accountability is paramount for trust in a democratically governed society, the vision of AI & Law is one of AI supporting human decision makers rather than replacing or unduly influencing them, and predictive systems should not be directly applied within courts. [CLM-0014-016]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Janeček (2023) argue that Because the justice system and court judgments are publicly funded resources, the public have an undisputable mandate to demand how court data are accessed and used, for example by requiring that judgments be available as bulk data only for a public benefit, as the DSM Directive does for text and data mining. The question to ask is whether this public mandate concerns both 'factual' and 'jurisprudential' judgments; if it does not, new reasons for limiting or not limiting access to all judgment records as bulk data must be identified and the public must be made to understand the distinction, otherwise access to judgments risks becoming much more restricted than it needs to be. [CLM-0041-013]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Gridin (2026) argue that The maturation of AI under the Rule of Law cannot be achieved through governance frameworks or legislative policy alone; it requires the codification of legal obligation directly into software architecture (a principle termed 'jurisprudential engineering'), so that compliance becomes a function of code rather than of policy. [CLM-0049-001]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Predictive

**CoE**

- Gridin (2026) argue that The Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (CETS No. 225) is the most ambitious attempt yet to establish a universal normative floor for AI architectural choices, extending human-rights obligations beyond the EU's boundaries to any state party regardless of its domestic regulatory model; the interaction between the Convention's obligations and the architectural choices of deploying entities will become the defining jurisprudential frontier of the next decade. [CLM-0049-049]. — jurisdiction: CoE; basis: legislation

**US**

- Miller (2025) argue that The utility of generative AI models for reinforcing legal stability is entirely dependent on the strategies by which they are employed; an interpreter who merely makes a quick query for a single ordinary meaning will likely derive far less useful information than one with a considered research strategy and, without due care, might be more likely to erode legal stability than reinforce it. [CLM-0039-021]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**general**

- Neumann et al. (2026) argue that Governmental actors may use procurement power or regulatory authority as commercial and political leverage to demand that providers remove, weaken, or add safety restrictions encoded in system-level instructions, thereby influencing the normative values encoded into deployed models. [CLM-0050-021]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Methodological

**general**

- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that LLM AIs introduce significantly more discretion than best-practice corpus analysis, not less: in corpus linguistics the discretionary decisions (corpus selection, operationalization of the construct, searches evaluable for precision and recall, coding frameworks evaluated for inter-rater reliability, statistical analysis) are informed by linguistic theory and can be transparently reported and replicated, whereas chatbot users can make none of these decisions, cannot know how the chatbot operationalizes constructs or evaluate it for accuracy, and depend on prompt engineering that is highly discretionary and highly influential. [CLM-0021-024]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
