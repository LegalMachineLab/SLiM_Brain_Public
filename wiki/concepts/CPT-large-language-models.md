# Large language models

_Status: anchor; family: technique_class._

## Definition

Large pre-trained generative language models such as GPT-3, GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and Llama used by prompting or fine-tuning.

Conceptual claims on this concept, each with its source:
- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025): An LLM is 'stable' if it reaches the same conclusion when asked the identical question multiple times. Stability on a question is measured as the number of calls returning the most-returned answer divided by the total number of calls (ranging between 50% and 100% for binary questions), and a model is unstable on a question if and only if its observed stability on that question is below 100%. [CLM-0016-002]
- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025): Stability is distinct from confidence: confidence describes how certain an LLM is that a single answer it gives is correct, whereas stability describes how dispersed an LLM's answers are across multiple calls with identical input. [CLM-0016-003]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): LLM chatbot responses to ordinary-meaning questions are neither a corpus linguistic analysis of natural language use nor a survey of human intuitions about ordinary meaning; at most they present the intuitions of a single artificial entity, functioning as an artificial expert witness that simulates one well-read human, and those artificial intuitions are no more transparent, replicable, or generalizable than the intuitions of an ordinary person or a judge. [CLM-0021-002]
- Lee and Egbert (2025): Serial prompts to one chatbot do not amount to a survey of separate speakers: any given AI rests on a single LLM, one 'brain', so variation across its responses reflects the variability of a single person repeatedly asked the same question, not the variability between individuals in a population; proponents must decide whether a single response is the (black-box) empirical result or whether multiple responses form a sample, since it cannot work both ways and both approaches present serious problems. [CLM-0021-019]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Direct querying of LLMs creates a risk of 'prompt shopping' (or 'prejudiced prompting') parallel to dictionary shopping: by merely adjusting how queries are framed, an interpreter can, consciously or unwittingly, elicit substantially different interpretations of the same legal text, and the current language-modelling paradigm offers no inherent mechanism to mitigate this. There is therefore no compelling reason to believe that LLMs, as employed through the direct-query method, offer any meaningful advantage over traditional interpretive tools in managing bias; they may simply add a new vector for selective interpretation, undermining the promised objectivity, consistency, predictability and transparency. [CLM-0022-009]
- Nay et al. (2023): Although the experiments are limited to U.S. tax law, the capabilities they investigate, finding relevant legal authorities and applying them to specific factual scenarios, are at the heart of legal work and could be generalised to other areas of legal practice. [CLM-0024-012]
- Nay et al. (2023): Methods that improve LLMs' legal analysis skills are relevant to aligning AI with humans and governing AI: an LLM that grasps the law could 'self-police' to act in accordance with law, or separate models could apply legal and ethical standards to confirm whether another AI is properly aligned with the law (the 'Law Informs Code' approach). [CLM-0024-016]
- Li et al. (2024): Evaluation of LLMs in the legal domain should be organized around the hierarchy and connections of cognitive abilities rather than solely by task difficulty or in a discrete manner; a Legal Cognitive Ability Taxonomy (LexAbility Taxonomy), inspired by Bloom's taxonomy and real-world legal application scenarios, categorizes LLM legal applications into six ability levels: Memorization, Understanding, Logic Inference, Discrimination, Generation, and Ethic. [CLM-0030-005]
- Hou et al. (2025): Applications of large language models to legal tasks divide into two kinds: fine-tuned legal LLMs, and LLM-based frameworks that leverage existing LLMs within a task framework without training them. [CLM-0034-002]
- Hou et al. (2025): The sources of synthetic supervised fine-tuning data for legal LLMs fall into five main types: existing traditional datasets, legal consultation websites, legal case documents, legal exams, and legal QA websites. [CLM-0034-007]
- Ovcharov (2026): The direction of the few-shot prompting effect on a legal task is hypothesised to depend on the ratio of format signal (what is learnable from examples) to length cost (prompt inflation from tokenizer fertility): judgment form classification has high format signal from distinctive document headers, whereas case-outcome prediction has low format signal because outcomes depend on factual reasoning rather than surface patterns. [CLM-0035-006]
- Miller (2025): Generative AI's utility exceeds that of dictionaries in its ability to address basic textual nuance, such as idioms, metaphors, and composite phrases like "physically restrained", and to grapple with ambiguity through inter-word relationships, whereas dictionaries may only be equipped to shed light on the isolated meaning of individual words. [CLM-0039-020]
- Hammond (2023): To understand what generative AI technologies really are and how they can and should be used, one needs to distinguish between skill in how to say things and knowledge of what to say. [CLM-0042-002]
- Gridin (2026): LLM hallucinations are not a software bug but a mathematical certainty arising from the probabilistic token-prediction architecture of large language models; a hallucination is the system working exactly as designed, and the failure lies with the human operator who expects a probabilistic generator to act as a deterministic fact-checker. [CLM-0049-002]
- Gridin (2026): Even at a temperature of zero an LLM remains a probabilistic synthesizer rather than a search engine, and in extended legal workflows context window collapse causes the model to forget its initial instructions, lose coherence, and hallucinate at an exponentially increasing rate; after roughly 20 to 30 complex queries commercial LLMs suffer severe degradation of continuity and logical coherence. [CLM-0049-003]
- Neumann et al. (2026): The goals that researchers attribute to system-level instructions fall into eight categories of two types: six system goals that target the behaviour of the AI system itself (alignment, accessibility, adaptability, performance, stability, security) and two prompt goals that target the instructions themselves as artefacts (implementation, auditability). [CLM-0050-002]

### Normative

### Conceptual

**general**

- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025) argue that An LLM is 'stable' if it reaches the same conclusion when asked the identical question multiple times. Stability on a question is measured as the number of calls returning the most-returned answer divided by the total number of calls (ranging between 50% and 100% for binary questions), and a model is unstable on a question if and only if its observed stability on that question is below 100%. [CLM-0016-002]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025) argue that Stability is distinct from confidence: confidence describes how certain an LLM is that a single answer it gives is correct, whereas stability describes how dispersed an LLM's answers are across multiple calls with identical input. [CLM-0016-003]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: literature
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that LLM chatbot responses to ordinary-meaning questions are neither a corpus linguistic analysis of natural language use nor a survey of human intuitions about ordinary meaning; at most they present the intuitions of a single artificial entity, functioning as an artificial expert witness that simulates one well-read human, and those artificial intuitions are no more transparent, replicable, or generalizable than the intuitions of an ordinary person or a judge. [CLM-0021-002]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Lee and Egbert (2025) argue that Serial prompts to one chatbot do not amount to a survey of separate speakers: any given AI rests on a single LLM, one 'brain', so variation across its responses reflects the variability of a single person repeatedly asked the same question, not the variability between individuals in a population; proponents must decide whether a single response is the (black-box) empirical result or whether multiple responses form a sample, since it cannot work both ways and both approaches present serious problems. [CLM-0021-019]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Direct querying of LLMs creates a risk of 'prompt shopping' (or 'prejudiced prompting') parallel to dictionary shopping: by merely adjusting how queries are framed, an interpreter can, consciously or unwittingly, elicit substantially different interpretations of the same legal text, and the current language-modelling paradigm offers no inherent mechanism to mitigate this. There is therefore no compelling reason to believe that LLMs, as employed through the direct-query method, offer any meaningful advantage over traditional interpretive tools in managing bias; they may simply add a new vector for selective interpretation, undermining the promised objectivity, consistency, predictability and transparency. [CLM-0022-009]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Nay et al. (2023) argue that Although the experiments are limited to U.S. tax law, the capabilities they investigate, finding relevant legal authorities and applying them to specific factual scenarios, are at the heart of legal work and could be generalised to other areas of legal practice. [CLM-0024-012]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Nay et al. (2023) argue that Methods that improve LLMs' legal analysis skills are relevant to aligning AI with humans and governing AI: an LLM that grasps the law could 'self-police' to act in accordance with law, or separate models could apply legal and ethical standards to confirm whether another AI is properly aligned with the law (the 'Law Informs Code' approach). [CLM-0024-016]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

[CLM-0030-005]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

- Miller (2025) argue that Generative AI's utility exceeds that of dictionaries in its ability to address basic textual nuance, such as idioms, metaphors, and composite phrases like "physically restrained", and to grapple with ambiguity through inter-word relationships, whereas dictionaries may only be equipped to shed light on the isolated meaning of individual words. ## Disagreements
