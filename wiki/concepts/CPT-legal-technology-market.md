# Legal technology market

_Status: emergent; family: other._

## Definition

The market for legal technology and legal AI products: vendors, incumbents, competitive dynamics, business models and defensibility (motivating claims: CLM-0040-004, CLM-0040-006, CLM-0040-010).

Conceptual claims on this concept, each with its source:
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Traditional SaaS provides structured, rules-based digital tools that humans navigate manually to generate an output, whereas AI-native or AI-enabled systems act as autonomous or semi-autonomous collaborators that understand human intent, execute complex workflows independently and deliver outcomes; among the latter, AI-native systems built from scratch with AI at their core have an edge over AI-enabled legacy platforms that add AI features onto an existing codebase. [CLM-0040-002]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Product moats for vertical AI applications can be categorised into five levels of increasing defensibility, ranging from surface-level utility to deep algorithmic differentiation: (A) workflows and UX (low), (B) the vertical harness and custom tools (medium), (C) built-in compliance (medium-high), (D) the Brain or data-driven operating system (high), and (E) embedded judgment (highest); developing one or more of these is critical for a vertical application to compete against old and new players. [CLM-0040-007]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Encapsulating company-specific workflows, usability, brand and taste as 'skills' (experts-in-a-box) is a genuine but low and narrow moat: foundation models will not learn these workflows because they do not generalise, yet with concerted investment competitors can replicate the same skills, so workflows and UX would typically not be the only standalone moat of a successful application-layer company. [CLM-0040-008]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): A vertical harness — an orchestrator custom-built around the vocabulary, artifacts and control flow of a specific industry, with specialised tools and integrations into legacy systems — is a medium moat: it is more valuable than a horizontal harness hosting a vertical workflow because it provides greater reliability, security and efficiency, and legacy-system integrations create significant durable value, but it is maintainable largely through engineering effort that a well-funded rival can match over time. [CLM-0040-009]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Built-in compliance is a medium-to-high, sticky moat for a vertical application: beyond the table-stakes base layer, meeting complex regulatory and policy compliance requirements with ongoing conformance guarantees means supplying the determinism, explainability and auditability that foundation models lack; horizontal players rarely absorb this burden, and once a firm wires compliance into its supervision and recordkeeping stack, switching means re-validating from scratch. [CLM-0040-012]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Compliance requirements facing a vertical application fall on a spectrum of three tiers — a strict liability tier of rules requiring absolute adherence on pain of criminal liability or catastrophic fines (such as GDPR, HIPAA or the EU AI Act's prohibited and high-risk categories), a tolerable penalty tier where non-compliance is a manageable business cost, and a cautionary or advisory tier with opportunities to correct and minimal penalties — and verticals and workflows in the more stringent tiers can create much deeper moats through specialised solutions. [CLM-0040-013]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): The 'Brain' or data-driven operating system — the encapsulation of proprietary data (clean curated public data, private customer data, real-time data, physical data and operational insights) into a vertical-specific algorithm for a specific use case — is a high moat: such data is not in a model's pre-training data and is difficult to curate, the moat lies in the curation process rather than in the foundation models themselves, and each customer's accumulated context compounds, raising switching costs the longer the system is used. [CLM-0040-016]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Embedded judgment — encoding the judgment, taste and experience of expert practitioners as business logic that guides decisions at critical points in complex multi-step workflows — is the highest moat: although very hard to codify today, it can be learned through a flywheel in which the system observes the decisions of the best practitioners and refines its own judgment, so the moat compounds with every additional customer and observed decision and becomes increasingly difficult for competitors to replicate. [CLM-0040-017]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Operational moats — distribution excellence, engineering excellence and operational excellence — sit outside the product moat framework: any company can achieve one and an operational moat cannot stand on its own as a defensible differentiator for a vertical application, but operational moats enhance and compound product moats against competitors. [CLM-0040-020]

Aliases: legal vertical, legal tech industry.

## Claims about the concept

### Descriptive

**GB**

- Janeček (2023) state that Because judgments in England and Wales do not emerge from any standardised production pipeline with a transparent audit trail, a gap has arisen that is filled by commercial legal publishers, who have secured privileged access to judgments (through transcription agencies, court administrators, or early access to scattered copies) and now commercially exploit that access; research suggests these publishers already control access to a vast majority of recorded judgments. [CLM-0041-004]. — jurisdiction: GB; basis: literature
- Janeček (2023) state that The public's concerns about the sensitivity of information contained in judgments play into the hands of commercial legal publishers, because it is not in the publishers' economic interest for more judgments to become available: wider availability would set back their competitive advantage and open the legal research services market to new entrants. [CLM-0041-007]. — jurisdiction: GB; basis: argument
- Briggs of Westbourne (2026) state that AI has rapidly gained prominence within the legal profession, emerging around 2023-2024 and becoming increasingly normalised from around 2025: virtually every law firm, and perhaps most barristers' chambers, are embracing some form of bespoke legal AI tool, and the courts are just starting to follow suit. [CLM-0047-005]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: none_stated; temporal reference: 2023 to 2026

**RU**

- Gridin (2026) state that In the Russian Federation, semiconductor sanctions and hardware constraints have driven leading technology conglomerates and financial institutions (MTS Web Services' MWS AI Agents Platform, Sberbank's multi-agent cash-collection and cybersecurity systems, GigaChat Enterprise) to adopt linear multi-agent architectures, producing a de facto convergence with the Neuro-Symbolic Sandwich model achieved through economic necessity rather than regulatory design; the architecture is therefore a technically neutral infrastructure belonging to no regulatory paradigm. [CLM-0049-044]. — jurisdiction: RU; basis: literature

**US**

- Mandal and Sinha (2026) state that In wealth management, which is governed by overlapping regimes of SEC recordkeeping and fiduciary rules, FINRA supervision and communications rules for broker-dealers, and state-level RIA rules, compliance rather than note-taking is the moat of the advisor productivity tool Jump, and compliance is what allows Jump to command a price premium over horizontal recording tools. [CLM-0040-014]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**general**

- Mandal and Sinha (2026) state that A vertical application-layer company can now expect to compete against a combination of new and old competitors enabled by foundation model capabilities: AI-native startups, AI-enabled traditional SaaS businesses, AI-enabled or AI-native services businesses, open source AI applications, and, where a vertical offers a compelling addressable market, foundation models themselves. [CLM-0040-001]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

**undetermined**

- Mandal and Sinha (2026) state that The legal vertical is a prime example of the entry of the new breed of AI competitors and shows the squeeze a vertical application may feel: alongside SaaS incumbents, fast-growing AI-native legal startups such as Harvey, Legora and Eudia, law firms building proprietary model solutions, AI-native law firms, an open source legal AI movement, and the foundation model providers Anthropic and OpenAI have all entered the legal market. [CLM-0040-004]. — jurisdiction: undetermined; basis: literature
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) state that The legal SaaS incumbents Thomson Reuters, LexisNexis and Wolters Kluwer retain a strong grip on exclusive access to primary law, case law and regulatory content, and have begun building their own AI-enabled or agentic solutions on that proprietary data, by themselves or by acquiring AI-native startups, and sharing their legal data through MCPs. [CLM-0040-006]. — jurisdiction: undetermined; basis: literature
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) state that In legal AI the moat is not the underlying model, since GPT, Claude or Gemini can often be used interchangeably; it lies partly in the harness — the layer that pulls matter context, applies legal-specific reasoning, calls trusted legal tools in order and writes work product back into the firm's systems under professional governance rules — and in the curated legal tools and document-management, research and content integrations that create a barrier to entry. [CLM-0040-010]. — jurisdiction: undetermined; basis: argument

### Interpretive

**general**

- Mandal and Sinha (2026) read Legal technology market as follows: When foundation models emerged in 2022, the legal domain lent itself especially well to innovation with large language models because of its text-heavy character and its use of language as its source code, relying on structured syntax, precedents and predictable rules. [CLM-0040-005]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

### Normative

**GB**

- Janeček (2023) argue that Private legal publishers are no longer in any legitimate position to determine access and usage rights over court judgments: whereas in the pre-digital era their control could be justified because their law reports were often the only record of a judgment, now that the law can be reported directly through electronic copies of judgments produced by publicly funded courts, it is difficult to justify the publishers' claim to control access to judgments. [CLM-0041-005]. — jurisdiction: GB; basis: argument
- Janeček (2023) argue that Because computational analysis does not engage with judgments in the jurisprudential sense, it is difficult to justify control over access to judgments as bulk data by reference to copyright, regardless of whether the access and usage rights over 'jurisprudential' judgments are taken to be determined by judges, parties' counsel and solicitors, private publishers, The National Archives, BAILII, the UK Government, or those who first obtain a judgment transcription. [CLM-0041-010]. — jurisdiction: GB [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

### Predictive

**general**

- Hartung et al. (2026) argue that Because large language models have fully entered public perception, players historically disinterested in Legal NLP research, such as publishing houses, law firms and courts, now have viable commercial interest in it; possessing vast data collections, they might be increasingly willing to share them with researchers, which is likely to lead to an uptick in research using real-world, commercial, or administrative datasets. [CLM-0023-021]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Hartung et al. (2026) argue that Within the larger world of legal technology, language-centric technologies are likely to play an ever-increasing role, and public players will likely focus on deployment in the context of digital justice, leveraging neural-era models to reduce case backlogs, improve access, and develop the ability to deliver justice at scale. [CLM-0023-022]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) argue that Despite the unbroken rise of foundation model capability, significant opportunities remain for vertical applications in knowledge-intensive industries such as legal, accounting, consulting, financial services, banking, education, healthcare and life sciences: vertical specialisation is a smaller niche market but can have a deeper moat and less of a competitive race than foundation model building, and vertical applications built to benefit from rising model capability can create durable, strong businesses. [CLM-0040-003]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) argue that Foundation model providers can build a vertical harness themselves: if incentives align, Anthropic or OpenAI could differentiate in a vertical such as legal by pursuing this route directly or indirectly via partnership. [CLM-0040-011]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) argue that With continued advancement of foundation models, vertical application solutions could next use their acquired judgment to solve open-ended problems without human direction — including recursively rebuilding the application to meet users' needs without human feedback — so that the application would rely on its own rather than human judgment to evolve and differentiate itself; this is not yet a reality but could be in the foreseeable future. [CLM-0040-019]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: literature
- Mandal and Sinha (2026) argue that As foundation model capability rises, the value of any single workflow, tool or product shipped in isolation tends to fall and become less defensible, whereas the value of a system combining workflows, custom tools and harness, built-in compliance, the Brain and embedded judgment does not fall: for these moats the sum is greater than the parts. [CLM-0040-021]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Chalkidis et al. (2021) argue that A standard, publicly available legal NLP benchmark would speed up the adoption and transparent evaluation of new legal NLP methods in the commercial sector, where many press releases announce high-performing systems but almost no independent evaluation of machine learning and NLP-based tools exists; it would also allay concerns of undue influence in predictive models, including the use of metadata which the relevant law expressly disregards. [CLM-0048-009]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
