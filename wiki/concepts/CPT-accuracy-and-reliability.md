# Accuracy and reliability

_Status: anchor; family: normative_concern._

## Definition

Correctness, stability, hallucination, and dependability of AI outputs in legal use.

Conceptual claims on this concept, each with its source:
- Tan et al. (2024): Determining whether two sentences make the same point is a more difficult task for legal text than for other text such as news, because legal texts involve complex reasoning and long legal documents sometimes require contextual understanding of the whole case to decide whether two sentences make the same point. [CLM-0015-004]
- Tan et al. (2024): The pointwise evaluation methodology focuses on the content of a summary and does not account for more subjective aspects of a text such as writing style and flow, which are nevertheless an important part of a well-written legal summary. [CLM-0015-017]
- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025): An LLM is 'stable' if it reaches the same conclusion when asked the identical question multiple times. Stability on a question is measured as the number of calls returning the most-returned answer divided by the total number of calls (ranging between 50% and 100% for binary questions), and a model is unstable on a question if and only if its observed stability on that question is below 100%. [CLM-0016-002]
- Blair-Stanek and Van Durme (2025): Stability is distinct from confidence: confidence describes how certain an LLM is that a single answer it gives is correct, whereas stability describes how dispersed an LLM's answers are across multiple calls with identical input. [CLM-0016-003]
- Waldon et al. (2025): Direct querying of LLMs creates a risk of 'prompt shopping' (or 'prejudiced prompting') parallel to dictionary shopping: by merely adjusting how queries are framed, an interpreter can, consciously or unwittingly, elicit substantially different interpretations of the same legal text, and the current language-modelling paradigm offers no inherent mechanism to mitigate this. There is therefore no compelling reason to believe that LLMs, as employed through the direct-query method, offer any meaningful advantage over traditional interpretive tools in managing bias; they may simply add a new vector for selective interpretation, undermining the promised objectivity, consistency, predictability and transparency. [CLM-0022-009]
- Mandal and Sinha (2026): Operational moats — distribution excellence, engineering excellence and operational excellence — sit outside the product moat framework: any company can achieve one and an operational moat cannot stand on its own as a defensible differentiator for a vertical application, but operational moats enhance and compound product moats against competitors. [CLM-0040-020]
- Hammond (2023): To understand what generative AI technologies really are and how they can and should be used, one needs to distinguish between skill in how to say things and knowledge of what to say. [CLM-0042-002]
- Gridin (2026): LLM hallucinations are not a software bug but a mathematical certainty arising from the probabilistic token-prediction architecture of large language models; a hallucination is the system working exactly as designed, and the failure lies with the human operator who expects a probabilistic generator to act as a deterministic fact-checker. [CLM-0049-002]
- Gridin (2026): Even at a temperature of zero an LLM remains a probabilistic synthesizer rather than a search engine, and in extended legal workflows context window collapse causes the model to forget its initial instructions, lose coherence, and hallucinate at an exponentially increasing rate; after roughly 20 to 30 complex queries commercial LLMs suffer severe degradation of continuity and logical coherence. [CLM-0049-003]
- Gridin (2026): Deterministic linear models such as decision trees, logistic regression, and rule-based expert systems cannot hallucinate, because their logic is hardcoded and observable; they can only evaluate input data against pre-defined factual datasets, and so provide absolute transparency at the cost of the linguistic creativity and processing capacity of an LLM. [CLM-0049-004]

## Claims about the concept

### Descriptive

**CZ**

- Novotná and Harašta (2025) state that The keywords manually assigned to Czech Constitutional Court decisions by court staff in the NALUS database, although treated as a gold standard, carry noise and inconsistency that grow over time because of staff turnover, the absence of formal assignment rules, and emerging legal issues; this problem is well known in Czechia, but its impact, including on automation, remains understudied. [CLM-0017-003]. — jurisdiction: CZ; basis: dataset_or_experiment

**UA**

- Ovcharov (2026) state that Ukrainian legal text is hard for large language models because three factors compound: subword tokenizers fragment Ukrainian legal text at rates about 1.6 times higher than English, inflating prompt length and reducing effective context; Ukrainian morphological richness (seven cases, three genders, synthetic verb forms) creates surface variation invisible to English-trained models; and the civil-law reasoning pattern of statute application, rather than case precedent, differs from the common-law data that dominates LLM pretraining corpora, so that English benchmarks do not capture the reasoning it requires. [CLM-0035-003]. — jurisdiction: UA; basis: argument

**US**

- Waldon et al. (2025) argue that Judges should not rely on direct queries to LLM chatbots (questions of the form 'what is the ordinary meaning of [term]?') as evidence of the ordinary meaning of legal texts; the direct-query method, as currently practised, is unsound.
