# UA-Legal-Bench: A Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models on Ukrainian Legal Reasoning

_Ovcharov, Volodymyr (2026), unknown (not printed in the text). Source file: `raw/W0841__UA-Legal-Bench_A_Benchmark_for_Evaluating_Large_Language_Models_on_Ukr.md`._

## What the source argues

Ovcharov (2026) argues that legal NLP benchmarks are English-centric and common-law dominated [CLM-0035-001], introduces UA-Legal-Bench as the first Ukrainian legal reasoning benchmark [CLM-0035-002], and explains why Ukrainian legal text is hard for LLMs [CLM-0035-003]. Across seven frontier and several smaller models the five tasks form a difficulty gradient [CLM-0035-004], few-shot effects are sharply task-dependent [CLM-0035-005], hypothesised to follow a format-signal to length-cost ratio [CLM-0035-006]; accuracy is dangerously misleading on imbalanced outcome prediction [CLM-0035-007], no model dominates so single-task evaluation misleads [CLM-0035-008], scaling effects are task-dependent [CLM-0035-009], few-shot prompting can equalise scale [CLM-0035-010], norm extraction is where small models match frontier ones [CLM-0035-011], English prompts degrade results [CLM-0035-012], the Ukrainian-English gap is task-dependent [CLM-0035-013], automatic labels agree moderately with an LLM judge [CLM-0035-014], and the source calls for multilingual benchmarks with class-aware metrics [CLM-0035-015].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- Ovcharov (2026) state that Existing legal NLP benchmarks are overwhelmingly English-centric: the major benchmarks (LegalBench, LexGLUE, CUAD) are English-only and predominantly common-law, and multilingual efforts (LEXTREME, MultiLegalPile) cover EU languages but exclude Cyrillic-script jurisdictions and civil-law systems outside Western Europe, so failure modes in morphologically rich, non-Latin-script languages go undetected. [CLM-0035-001]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: literature
- Ovcharov (2026) state that No prior benchmark exists for Ukrainian legal reasoning; UA-Legal-Bench is the first legal reasoning benchmark for a Cyrillic-script, civil-law jurisdiction. [CLM-0035-002]. — jurisdiction: UA; basis: literature
- Ovcharov (2026) state that Ukrainian legal text is hard for large language models because three factors compound: subword tokenizers fragment Ukrainian legal text at rates about 1.6 times higher than English, inflating prompt length and reducing effective context; Ukrainian morphological richness (seven cases, three genders, synthetic verb forms) creates surface variation invisible to English-trained models; and the civil-law reasoning pattern of statute application, rather than case precedent, differs from the common-law data that dominates LLM pretraining corpora, so that English benchmarks do not capture the reasoning it requires. [CLM-0035-003]. — jurisdiction: UA; basis: argument

### Normative

- Ovcharov (2026) argue that Legal NLP evaluation needs multilingual legal benchmarks with appropriate, class-aware metrics, because patterns such as accuracy masking majority-class prediction and few-shot prompting compensating for model scale are invisible in English-only, accuracy-only evaluation. [CLM-0035-015]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Empirical

- Ovcharov (2026) report that On Ukrainian court-decision tasks, the five UA-Legal-Bench tasks form a clear difficulty gradient for large language models: case-type classification is nearly solved (all frontier models exceed 96% zero-shot accuracy), judgment form classification is substantially harder (74-84% zero-shot), cause category prediction sits between (44-51% accuracy), and case-outcome prediction from masked facts is the hardest task (frontier models reach only 23-41% macro-F1 zero-shot). [CLM-0035-004]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that Few-shot prompting effects on Ukrainian legal tasks are sharply task-dependent and cannot be predicted from task difficulty alone: 3-shot prompting consistently helps judgment form classification (six of seven frontier models gain 8.6-17.7 pp), has mixed effects on case-outcome prediction (macro-F1 rises for most models but falls for some), gives modest consistent gains on cause category prediction (+2.2 to +5.6 pp), has negligible effect on norm extraction, and is minimal on case-type classification because of a ceiling effect. Few-shot prompting can therefore simultaneously help and hurt the same model on different tasks, and legal AI practitioners cannot assume it will improve performance without measuring the effect per task. [CLM-0035-005]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that Within-family scaling effects on Ukrainian legal tasks are highly task-dependent and the parameter threshold for competence varies dramatically across model families: larger models do not reliably outperform smaller ones, 8B models suffice for simpler tasks (case-type classification, norm extraction), and on harder tasks (outcome prediction, cause category) scaling matters but Mistral's 8B model is competitive with its 675B sibling while Meta's 8B model is not. These results challenge the assumption that legal AI requires frontier-scale models. [CLM-0035-009]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that Few-shot prompting can act as a scale equalizer, partially compensating for a tenfold parameter deficit on format-learnable legal tasks: with 3-shot prompting a 12B model rises from 51.8% to 90.4% on Ukrainian judgment form classification (+38.6 pp), nearly matching its 120B sibling, so that a small model with few-shot examples reaches near-frontier accuracy at far lower cost than a large model zero-shot. [CLM-0035-010]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that Legal norm extraction from Ukrainian court decisions is the only benchmark task on which small models match frontier performance (F1 0.318-0.391 across all models), and few-shot prompting has negligible effect on it, suggesting that norm extraction relies on pattern recognition rather than deep legal reasoning. [CLM-0035-011]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that Case-outcome prediction results on Ukrainian decisions are stable across Ukrainian prompt variants, but translating the prompt into English causes moderate degradation for one model and a surprising improvement for another, suggesting that prompt language interacts with model architecture in non-trivial ways. [CLM-0035-012]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that The performance gap between Ukrainian and English legal benchmarks for frontier LLMs is task-dependent rather than uniform across all legal reasoning: Ukrainian case-type classification results (96-98%) are consistent with the above-90% accuracy frontier models reach on comparable LegalBench tasks, whereas Ukrainian case-outcome prediction (23-41% macro-F1) and cause category prediction (44-55%) show substantially more variance. [CLM-0035-013]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) report that The automatically derived ground-truth labels of UA-Legal-Bench agree with an independent LLM judge at 70% for case outcomes and 58% for cause categories; the moderate cause-category agreement reflects the inherent ambiguity of topic classification, since many cases span multiple categories, rather than systematic labeling errors, while the roughly 30% noise in outcome labels may attenuate measured model differences. [CLM-0035-014]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Conceptual

- Ovcharov (2026) argue that The direction of the few-shot prompting effect on a legal task is hypothesised to depend on the ratio of format signal (what is learnable from examples) to length cost (prompt inflation from tokenizer fertility): judgment form classification has high format signal from distinctive document headers, whereas case-outcome prediction has low format signal because outcomes depend on factual reasoning rather than surface patterns. [CLM-0035-006]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: argument

### Methodological

- Ovcharov (2026) argue that Accuracy is a dangerously misleading metric on imbalanced legal classification tasks, and imbalanced legal benchmarks require class-aware metrics such as macro-F1: on Ukrainian case-outcome prediction (61% majority class) the model with the highest accuracy is a majority-class predictor with the lowest macro-F1 among frontier models, while macro-F1 identifies the model that genuinely distinguishes outcome classes. [CLM-0035-007]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Ovcharov (2026) argue that No single large language model dominates across all Ukrainian legal tasks, and model rankings are unstable across tasks; evaluating on a single legal task, as most prior benchmarks do, therefore provides an incomplete picture of model capability. [CLM-0035-008]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:UA; basis: dataset_or_experiment
