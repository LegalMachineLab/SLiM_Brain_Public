# LexEval: A Comprehensive Chinese Legal Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models

_Li, Haitao, Ai, Qingyao, Zhang, Ruizhe, Chen, You, Wu, Yueyue, Liu, Yiqun (2024), unknown (not printed in the text). Source file: `raw/W0607__LexEval_A_Comprehensive_Chinese_Legal_Benchmark_for_Evaluating_Large_L.md`._

## What the source argues

Li et al. (2024) argue that applying LLMs to legal systems without careful evaluation is risky and that a standardised benchmark is needed [CLM-0030-001], given LLMs' statistical basis, hallucination and bias [CLM-0030-002]; general benchmarks give little legal guidance [CLM-0030-003] and existing Chinese legal benchmarks are not comprehensive [CLM-0030-004], so evaluation should follow a taxonomy of legal cognitive abilities [CLM-0030-005] that extends beyond China [CLM-0030-006]. LexEval is presented as the largest Chinese legal benchmark [CLM-0030-007], mostly in multiple-choice form [CLM-0030-008]. Across 38 models GPT-4 is best yet far from satisfactory [CLM-0030-009], size helps [CLM-0030-010], chat models beat base models [CLM-0030-011], legal-specific models do not always win [CLM-0030-012], legal-evolution tasks are poorly handled [CLM-0030-013], few-shot gains are slight and unstable [CLM-0030-014] so in-context learning may not inject legal knowledge [CLM-0030-015], memorisation is the critical weakness [CLM-0030-016] not fixed by more legal corpora [CLM-0030-017], understanding and logic inference are strongest [CLM-0030-018], discrimination and generation weak [CLM-0030-019] and should exploit document structure [CLM-0030-020], and ethics tasks remain unsatisfactory [CLM-0030-021], calling for alignment and supervision [CLM-0030-022]. The source concludes that LLMs cannot yet solve Chinese legal problems [CLM-0030-023], calls for innovation and cooperation [CLM-0030-024], expects statute-law and case-law systems to differ [CLM-0030-025], flags ROUGE-L [CLM-0030-026] and document length [CLM-0030-027] as limits, and holds that LLMs should not replace professionals [CLM-0030-028] and that benchmark scores are only a reference [CLM-0030-029].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- Li et al. (2024) state that LexEval is, to the authors' knowledge, the largest and most comprehensive Chinese legal benchmark for evaluating LLMs, comprising 23 tasks and 14,150 questions drawn from reformatted existing legal datasets, real legal profession qualification examination questions, and datasets newly annotated by legal experts. [CLM-0030-007]. — jurisdiction: CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Interpretive

- Li et al. (2024) read Large language models as follows: The application of large language models in the legal domain raises three concerns: because LLMs derive decisions from statistical patterns in training data rather than professional knowledge and logical reasoning, they often fall short of ensuring reliability and explainability; they may produce misleading and factually incorrect content (hallucination) that could mislead legal practitioners; and they may reflect biases in their training data, leading to unfair treatment of certain groups and undermining the fairness of judicial proceedings. [CLM-0030-002]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: literature
- Li et al. (2024) read The legal NLP research field as follows: Existing general-purpose LLM evaluation benchmarks, such as C-Eval, provide limited guidance for the legal domain because they assess generalist abilities on non-professional or semi-professional texts and are unable to reflect or capture the complexity of judicial cognition and decision-making. [CLM-0030-003]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) read Legal data resources as follows: Existing Chinese legal LLM benchmarks built from traditional natural language processing datasets, such as LawBench and LaiW, do not provide a standardized and comprehensive evaluation: traditional datasets test specific capabilities from a computer-centric perspective that does not always reflect practical legal use of LLMs, these benchmarks often overlook legal ethics, and their evaluation metrics vary significantly. [CLM-0030-004]. — jurisdiction: CN; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) read Large language models as follows: In-context learning may not be an ideal way to inject legal knowledge into LLMs. [CLM-0030-015]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) read Large language models as follows: Merely increasing legal corpora during pre-training may not be the optimal solution to LLMs' weak memorization of legal knowledge, given that even legal-specific LLMs exhibit weaknesses at the Memorization level. [CLM-0030-017]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Normative

- Li et al. (2024) argue that Applying existing large language models to legal systems without careful evaluation of their potential and limitations could pose significant risks in legal practice, so a standardized and comprehensive benchmark is urgently needed to ensure that LLMs meet the high standards required for legal practice. [CLM-0030-001]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that To improve LLM performance at discerning and evaluating legal content and at generating well-formatted legal texts, the structured information within legal documents should be leveraged and more rational training objectives designed. [CLM-0030-020]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) argue that To address LLMs' unsatisfactory performance on ethics-related legal tasks, more advanced and precise alignment strategies should be devised, and the supervision and evaluation of LLMs should be strengthened to ensure they conform to ethical standards and moral requirements in practical applications. [CLM-0030-022]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that Continuous technological innovation and interdisciplinary cooperation are needed to bring about more powerful legal LLMs and improve the efficiency and quality of legal services. [CLM-0030-024]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that LLMs should not completely replace legal professionals in legal practice, because legal judgment is unique and complex, requiring rich professional knowledge, experience, and humanistic insight; the goal of benchmarking is to help legal professionals make more informed decisions on when, where, and how to use these technologies. [CLM-0030-028]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that Benchmark evaluation results for legal LLMs are only for reference and not the sole basis for decision-making; when applying large models in real-world legal scenarios, more in-depth or specific evaluations are still needed to ensure the legitimacy and rationality of decisions. [CLM-0030-029]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Empirical

- Li et al. (2024) report that On a comprehensive Chinese legal benchmark, the closed-source model GPT-4 achieves the best overall performance and open-source models perform slightly worse, but GPT-4 remains far from perfect on many tasks owing to its lack of knowledge related to the Chinese legal system, indicating significant room for improvement of LLMs in the legal domain. [CLM-0030-009]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Increasing model size leads to better performance on legal tasks, as it does in general domains; for example, Qwen-14B performs better than Qwen-7B on a Chinese legal benchmark. [CLM-0030-010]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that LLMs designed for chat and dialogue often perform better on legal tasks than their base models (for example Baichuan-13B-Chat over Baichuan-13B-base), an advantage that may stem from better instruction following and that suggests supervised fine-tuning and alignment optimization can significantly release the broader capabilities of LLMs. [CLM-0030-011]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Legal-specific LLMs do not always perform better than general LLMs on legal tasks; two speculated reasons are that legal-specific models are limited by base models weaker than models such as GPT-4, and that continuous pre-training on legal corpora may impair the abilities of the original base models, so appropriate training objectives for legal-specific LLMs still need to be designed. [CLM-0030-012]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Most LLMs, including models trained on legal data, perform poorly on legal evolution tasks and struggle to comprehend changes in legal norms across different periods; designing ways to make LLMs aware of the evolution of the law deserves further attention. [CLM-0030-013]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Under a few-shot setting, most LLMs show only slight and usually unstable performance improvements on legal tasks; the effect of few-shot examples varies across models, with some (such as GPT-4) improving and others (such as Qwen-14B-Chat) degrading, possibly because few-shot inputs become overly lengthy for certain models. [CLM-0030-014]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that LLMs perform poorly at the Memorization level of legal cognitive ability, which may be the critical obstacle to performing legal tasks at higher cognitive levels. [CLM-0030-016]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Most LLMs perform best at the Understanding and Logic Inference levels of legal cognitive ability: within a given context or when provided with the relevant legal provisions, LLMs can effectively use their inherent reasoning abilities to provide reasonable answers, although complex tasks such as multi-hop reasoning remain challenging. [CLM-0030-018]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that Current LLMs do not yet possess the ability to discern and evaluate legal content (the Discrimination level) and are inefficient at producing well-formatted legal texts (the Generation level), a limitation that arises primarily from the highly specialized and structured nature of legal texts. [CLM-0030-019]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that At the Ethic level of legal cognitive ability, even GPT-4, which shows relatively good performance, remains far from satisfactory, and the unsatisfactory performance of LLMs on ethics-related legal tasks poses serious challenges to their safe application in real-life scenarios. [CLM-0030-021]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that At present, LLMs cannot effectively solve legal problems under the Chinese legal system and are unable to provide effective legal assistance, even the high-performing GPT-4 included. [CLM-0030-023]. — jurisdiction: CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Li et al. (2024) report that The typically lengthy nature of legal documents poses a potential challenge for applying LLMs in the legal domain, since legal inputs such as similar case identification queries (average length 4,502 characters) can exceed the maximum input length of some LLMs. [CLM-0030-027]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:CN; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Conceptual

- Li et al. (2024) argue that Evaluation of LLMs in the legal domain should be organized around the hierarchy and connections of cognitive abilities rather than solely by task difficulty or in a discrete manner; a Legal Cognitive Ability Taxonomy (LexAbility Taxonomy), inspired by Bloom's taxonomy and real-world legal application scenarios, categorizes LLM legal applications into six ability levels: Memorization, Understanding, Logic Inference, Discrimination, Generation, and Ethic. [CLM-0030-005]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that Although the legal cognitive ability taxonomy is primarily designed for the Chinese legal system, it can be extended to legal tasks in other countries, because the six ability levels are universal across different legal systems. [CLM-0030-006]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Predictive

- Li et al. (2024) argue that Because statute law systems and case law systems differ significantly in the interpretation of laws and the basis for decisions, the performance of LLMs may differ between the two legal systems, so findings from a benchmark that mainly covers a statute law system require further in-depth exploration in case law systems. [CLM-0030-025]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument

### Methodological

- Li et al. (2024) argue that Converting all evaluation questions except generation tasks into multiple-choice format is a simple and effective way to evaluate LLM capabilities, because multiple-choice questions have a clearly defined metric (accuracy) and have been widely used in benchmarks. [CLM-0030-008]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
- Li et al. (2024) argue that Rouge-L, used as the main metric for legal text generation tasks, may not be able to fully and accurately present LLMs' performance in the legal domain. [CLM-0030-026]. — jurisdiction: general; basis: argument
