# Thinking Longer, Not Always Smarter: Evaluating LLM Capabilities in Hierarchical Legal Reasoning

_Zhang, Li, Gray, Morgan, Grabmair, Matthias, Ashley, Kevin (2026), CSLAW 26 (ACM Symposium on Computer Science and Law), March 2026, Berkeley (ACM reference block; doi:10.1145/3788646.3789522). Source file: `raw/W0876__Thinking_Longer_Not_Always_Smarter_Evaluating_LLM_Capabilities_in_Hier.md`._

## What the source argues

Zhang et al. (2026) formalise the identification of significant distinctions between a case and a precedent in the CATO tradition [CLM-0038-001], defining a distinction as significant when it can be emphasised and not downplayed [CLM-0038-002], and find that reasoning LLMs decline systematically as hierarchical legal reasoning gets more complex, collapsing on the integrated task [CLM-0038-003]; intermediate reasoning helps [CLM-0038-004], but models spend more tokens on wrong answers [CLM-0038-005], suggesting they struggle on hard instances [CLM-0038-006], with no strong effort-performance correlation [CLM-0038-007] and a verbosity trade-off in traces [CLM-0038-008]. They suggest the limitation also exists, obscured, in natural-language settings [CLM-0038-009], raise concerns for practice [CLM-0038-010], argue that structured decomposition localises failures better than isolated-skill benchmarks [CLM-0038-011], and question current post-training for legal reasoning [CLM-0038-012].

## Claims

### Normative

- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that Because the ability to identify significant distinctions is important to legal practice, the current inability of LLMs to perform this task reliably raises concerns about their readiness for real-world legal applications: effective legal AI must be capable of multi-step, multi-level abstraction and synthesis, and the current generation of LLMs, despite impressive surface-level capabilities, lacks the sophisticated reasoning mechanisms necessary for the nuanced analysis required in legal practice. [CLM-0038-010]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that Current post-training methods for reasoning LLMs, which typically rely on reinforcement learning on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks, have limitations in handling complex multi-step legal analysis and may not adequately prepare models for the domain-specific reasoning patterns of legal analysis; the systematic failures on integrated legal reasoning suggest the need for innovative post-training approaches, and developing specialised training datasets and post-training methodologies for legal reasoning, attentive to the hierarchical and multi-step nature of legal argumentation, might improve LLM reasoning capabilities in legal contexts. [CLM-0038-012]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: argument

### Empirical

- Zhang et al. (2026) report that Reasoning LLMs show a systematic decline in accuracy as the complexity of hierarchical legal reasoning tasks increases: all evaluated models reach 100% accuracy on identifying surface-level distinctions between cases (Task 1), accuracy degrades to 64.82%-92.09% on hierarchical reasoning about the argumentative roles of a distinction (Task 2), and collapses to 11.46%-33.99% on the integrated identification of all significant distinctions (Task 3), which suggests that the integration of multiple reasoning steps is a challenge for current reasoning LLMs. [CLM-0038-003]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) report that The intermediate reasoning process of thinking models, enabled by reinforcement-learning-based post-training, is beneficial for navigating the hierarchical structure of legal knowledge: the qwen3 thinking model reached 78.66% accuracy on analysing the argumentative roles of a distinction (Task 2) against 30.04% for its non-thinking counterpart, and retained 33.99% accuracy on identifying all significant distinctions (Task 3) where the non-thinking model scored 0.00%. [CLM-0038-004]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) report that Reasoning LLMs consistently expend more reasoning tokens on incorrect responses than on correct ones across multiple models and hierarchical legal reasoning tasks (for example, gpt-5 used 4,456 tokens on average for incorrect Task 2 responses against 3,081 for correct ones), suggesting that 'thinking longer' does not always mean 'thinking smarter'. [CLM-0038-005]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) report that The pattern of higher reasoning-token expenditure on incorrect responses suggests that, when reasoning LLMs encounter difficult hierarchical legal reasoning problems, they may fall into inefficient reasoning loops or extensive but unfocused analysis ('overthinking'), and its consistency across models suggests it may represent a limitation of current LLM reasoning mechanisms and post-training approaches: the models appear to lack effective strategies for recognising when they are on the wrong track and redirecting their reasoning. [CLM-0038-006]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) report that Across reasoning LLMs there is no strong correlation between reasoning effort and performance on hierarchical legal reasoning tasks (qwen3-thinking used 9,596 tokens on average for Task 2 with 78.66% accuracy, against gpt-5's 3,189 tokens and 92.09% accuracy). This challenges the assumption that more reasoning effort leads to better outcomes and suggests that the quality of reasoning matters more than its quantity, so that simply increasing reasoning length may not improve performance on reasoning tasks. [CLM-0038-007]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment
- Zhang et al. (2026) report that A qualitative comparison of reasoning traces on one instance of the task of identifying all significant distinctions reveals a trade-off: qwen3-thinking's verbose and repetitive process, while inefficient, was more methodical and applied the rules more accurately, whereas gpt-oss-120b's more concise, direct approach was brittle and missed a support path in the hierarchy, leading to an incorrect conclusion; this suggests that for complex rule-based reasoning a more deliberative process may be necessary to ensure accuracy. [CLM-0038-008]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: dataset_or_experiment

### Conceptual

- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that A distinction between a current case and a precedent is significant if and only if it can be emphasized and cannot be downplayed. A significant distinction is a fundamental factual difference between the cases that is not easily explained away; it points to a core weakness in the analogical argument and provides a strong basis for arguing for a different outcome. [CLM-0038-002]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument

### Predictive

- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that The core limitation in hierarchical reasoning that a formal factor-based representation exposes may also exist, but be obfuscated, when LLMs operate on contextual narrative legal work, especially in areas well represented in their pre-training data or accessible through retrieval-augmented generation: in such cases models might appear to reason effectively by retrieving and adapting existing solutions, masking an underlying deficit in hierarchical problem-solving. [CLM-0038-009]. — jurisdiction: geographical_proxy:US; basis: argument

### Methodological

- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that The process of identifying significant distinctions between a current case and a precedent in case-based legal reasoning can be formalised, in the CATO tradition, as a decomposed framework of three increasingly complex tasks: (1) identify distinctions, (2) analyse the argumentative roles of a distinction (emphasis versus downplaying) through a hierarchy of factors, legal concerns and legal issues, and (3) identify all significant distinctions. Cases are modelled as sets of factors, the hierarchy as a directed acyclic graph with strong and weak support edges, and a symbolic solver computes ground truth from formal rules about distinctions, support, blocking, and emphasis/downplay, bridging abstract legal theory and computational implementation. [CLM-0038-001]. — jurisdiction: US [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
- Zhang et al. (2026) argue that Existing legal AI benchmarks such as CUAD, LegalBench and COLIEE focus primarily on isolated skills rather than the integrated application of a reasoning chain; structuring evaluation around a principled, decomposed reasoning process instead of atomic components enables detailed analysis of where models succeed and fail and provides a methodology for fine-grained analysis of LLM reasoning capabilities in complex domains, identifying limitations before deployment. [CLM-0038-011]. — jurisdiction: general [jurisdiction inferred]; basis: argument
